We examine Higgsino dark matter (DM) as a potential explanation of the 248 keV nuclear-
recoil event of interest reported by the LUX-ZEPLIN (LZ) experiment, taking into account IceCube
constraints on DM annihilation in the Sun. In the nearly pure Higgsino limit, two neutral Majorana
states separated by a small mass splittingδare connected by an off-diagonal coupling to theZ
boson, providing a natural realization of inelastic DM. Using a likelihood analysis, we identify 90%
confidence regions compatible with the LZ event and consistent with the null results of IceCube
searches, both with and without including a constraint from the absence of events in the LZ high-
energy sideband. In our LZ analysis, we consider the DM distribution in the Standard Halo Model
alone as well as possible enhancements of the high-speed tail induced by the Large Magellanic Cloud
(LMC). We present four variants of bounds from IceCube on Higgsino dark matter, depending on
various assumptions on cooling channels in the Sun, motivated by severe theoretical uncertainties
about Higgsino-nucleon elastic scattering (unimportant for direct detection but crucial for IceCube
bounds). Whereas the thermal Higgsino with a mass of 1.1 TeV remains only marginally compatible
with both the LZ event and the IceCube limits when the LMC-induced high-speed tail is included
and both elastic cooling in the Sun and the LZ high-energy sideband constraint are neglected, higher
mass Higgsinos are excellent candidates for the LZ event in light of IceCube constraints. Depending
on the halo distribution, cooling interactions, and whether the LZ high-energy sideband is included,
the compatible regions in the Higgsino parameter space (m χ,δ) extend from masses of∼2 TeV to
hundreds of TeV or higher, with mass splittings ranging approximately from 350 to 525 keV.
I. INTRODUCTION
Although Dark Matter (DM) constitutes most (∼
85%) of the mass of the Universe, its nature remains a
mystery. One of the best-motivated candidates is Weakly
Interacting Massive Particles (WIMPs), which are pro-
posed to exist within several scenarios from Beyond the
Standard Model (BSM) of particle physics.
Direct detection laboratory experiments, originally
proposed in the 1980s [1, 2], are designed to probe the
interactions of WIMP dark matter with atomic nuclei in
terrestrial detectors situated deep underground. Despite
decades of experimental searches with continuously im-
proving sensitivity to rare dark matter interactions, the
particle nature of dark matter remains unsolved. Over
the years there have been hints of detection, but conclu-
sive discovery remains elusive. The DAMA/LIBRA ex-
periment observed an annual modulation of the signal in
NaI detectors consistent with a WIMP interpretation[3],
but these results are in tension with recent experiments
made of the same material [4].
Recently, a single unexplained event found by the
LUX-ZEPLIN (LZ) Collaboration has generated a great

particle interacting with the detector. The LZ experi-
ment, consisting of xenon nuclei with a 2.84 tonne-year
exposure, extended dark matter searches to a regime not

sure out to a higher nuclear-recoil energy range, with sen-

interest with a nuclear recoil of 248±23 (stat)±23 (sys)
keV. Rare background processes and detector effects were
studied in detail and shown not to be consistent with this
the event. To quantify its significance, they used a profile
likelihood ratio test, and found the background only hy-

with a maximum local significance of 3.4σfor the dark
matter models they tested. While more data will be re-
quired to confirm or rule out this hint of a discovery, its
potential importance motivates investigating theoretical
explanations of the event of interest.
Candidates for WIMP dark matter are found within
the context of supersymmetric (SUSY) extensions of the
Standard Model (SM) of particle physics. In our earlier
paper [6], we proposed Higgsino dark matter as a poten-
tial explanation for the LZ event. The Higgsino is the
SUSY partner of the Higgs boson of the SM. In Ref. [6],
we focused on the case of a thermal 1.1 TeV Higgsino.
In this paper, we generalize our studies to include the
possibility of higher higgsino masses.

Higgsinos are split into two mass eigenstates, with a small
energy differenceδbetween the masses. The lighter Hig-
gsino would be the dark matter. Tree level Z exchange
produces inelastic scattering of a Higgsino with a xenon
nucleus: the lighter Higgsino can be excited to the higher
mass state, giving the xenon nucleus a substantial kick
with a large recoil energy. In our earlier paper [6], we fo-
cused on the case of the∼1 TeV Higgsino, as LZ’s study
had investigated inelastic scattering of 1 TeV dark mat-
ter as an explanation of their data; additionally thermal
production of 1.1 TeV Higgsinos in the early universe
produces the correct relic abundance today to explain
the dark matter. Our paper showed that predictions for
a Higgsino with a mass of∼1 TeV, for a mass split-
ting around 350 keV, that has inelastic spin independent
(SI) scattering with the Xenon target, would fall within
the LZ two-sided confidence interval for their event of in-
terest. The following papers proposed the same idea of
Higgsino DM as explanation of the LZ event on the same
day we did: Refs. [9–11] (these papers studied specifically
∼1.1 TeV thermal Higgsinos). In the current paper, we
generalize our studies to include higher Higgsino masses,
as well as take into account important effects described
shortly. We will show that, although the 1 TeV Higgsino
remains only marginally viable in light of bounds from
IceCube as discussed below, Higgsinos of mass 2 TeV and
above can match data from all existing experiments.

fects came to light:
1) The original calculations only included DM particles
from the Standard Halo Model (SHM). The large mass

pay particular attention to the IceCube bounds, where we
perform a careful analysis that differs substantially from
that of many other authors. We begin with the case of
the 1.1 TeV WIMP, which would be thermally produced

the combination of the above bounds is difficult to ac-
commodate. Then we turn to arbitrary Higgsino mass
and mass splitting, and examine which regions of param-

IV. EFFECTS OF THE LARGE MAGELLENIC
CLOUD ON THE HIGH VELOCITY T AIL OF
THE DM DISTRIBUTION
The LZ analysis adopted the standard astrophysical
parameters recommended in Ref. [32], including the Stan-
dard Halo Model (SHM) velocity distribution. However,
high energy recoil events can be very sensitive to the
high-speed tail of the local DM distribution, which is
not well-known. The interaction of our dark matter halo
with neighbor halos, such as the Large Magellanic Cloud
(LMC) [33, 35], can accelerate the DM particles in our
halo and allow high-speed DM particles from neighboring
halos to enter ours as well as gravitationally significantly
enhance the high-speed tail of the local DM distribution.
Including the effects of the LMC is particularly impor-
tant as it weighs∼10−20% of the Milky Way. In our
earlier paper [6], we took into account only the WIMPs
from the SHM.
In our current analysis we explore two cases, a SHM
only case [32], and a SHM-plus-LMC case with the time-
averaged, present-day MW+LMC halo velocity distribu-
tion shown in Fig. 11 of Ref. [33]. Because the figure
provides no information beyondv min ≃950 km/s, we
conservatively setη(v min) = 0 above this value.
In this Section, we explore the effects of including the
LMC for the case of the thermal 1.1 TeV Higgsino, as an
extension of our earlier work. In subsequent sections, we
generalize to arbitrary Higgsino masses, with and with-
out the inclusion of LMC effects.

V. HIGH-ENERGY SIDEBAND IN LZ DA T A
Rodd et al. suggested that any explanation of the LZ
event atδ= 350 keV should also be consistent with the
lack of events in the LZ experiment at higher energies,
in its “sideband” at recoil energies∼350–650 keV [14].
However, the LZ collaboration has not published the ac-
ceptance in this sideband and did not include it in the
DM signal likelihood. The nuclear recoil response of the
detector up at those high recoil energies has not yet been
calibrated and is the subject of current work by the LZ
collaboration. Hence in this paper we will present results
both with and without the empty sideband taken into
account.
In those cases where we do require compatibilty of
our model with an empty sideband, we extend our LZ

VI. SOLAR BOUNDS FROM NEUTRINO
TELESCOPES
Neutrino telescopes provide complementary probes of
dark matter particles through searches for neutrinos from
DM annihilation in the Sun 7. In particular, the absence
of a neutrino excess from the direction of the Sun in Ice-
Cube and Super-Kamiokande places strong constraints
on the DM particles studied in this paper. The same
inelastic DM–nucleus scattering process that could ex-
plain the LZ event can also occur as DM traverses the
Sun. Inelastic interactions with solar nuclei cause DM
particles to lose energy, allowing some to become gravi-
tationally bound to the Sun when their speeds fall below
the local solar escape speed. Because the solar gravi-
tational field accelerates incident DM particles, inelastic
scattering on heavy solar nuclei can occur at mass split-
tings beyond the reach of terrestrial xenon experiments.
Once captured, DM particles can cool by losing energy
through repeated scattering with solar nuclei. Inelas-
tic cooling stops when scattering becomes kinematically
inaccessible, while elastic interactions can allow further
energy loss. This cooling concentrates the captured pop-
ulation closer to the solar center, increasing its density
and enhancing the annihilation rate. If the Higgsino in-
teractions are sufficiently effective, cooling can proceed
rapidly enough for Higgsino capture and annihilation to
approach equilibrium. Capture–annihilation equilibrium
implies that the number of Higgsinos captured per unit
time equals the number lost to annihilation, yielding the
maximum annihilation rate sustained by a capture rate.
Understanding when cooling becomes inefficient or ceases
is essential, as well as identifying whether or not capture–
annihilation equilibrium is reached. DM annihilation in
the Sun produces neutrinos that can be detected by neu-
trino telescopes. The absence of a DM-induced solar neu-
trino excess in IceCube can therefore constrain Higgsino
properties, specifically the particle mass and mass split-
ting [12, 13]. Here we estimate these constraints under
different assumptions about the interactions responsible

B. DM distribution in the Sun: cooling and
possible thermalization
To compute the DM annihilation rate in the Sun, we
need the spatial distribution of the captured DM pop-
ulation. Captured Higgsinos can lose energy through
repeated scattering on solar nuclei, sinking towards the
center of the Sun. The key question in this subsection
is the spatial distribution of DM after cooling. We ap-
proximate the resulting DM population as uniformly dis-
tributed within a sphere of radiusR, which we determine
in the remainder of this subsection.
A bound Higgsino traverses the Sun repeatedly, in-
creasing the probability of undergoing multiple scatter-
ings in the solar interior before annihilating. Several scat-
tering channels contribute to this cooling. First, Higgsi-
nos can continue to scatter through the inelastic SI inter-
action after capture. Cooling through this channel stops
when Higgsino energies fall below the kinematic thresh-
old for inelastic scattering, even on the heaviest solar
nuclei. As a Higgsino loses energy, the last species in our
solar composition on which it can scatter inelastically
is uranium. Following Ref. [12], we approximate the tra-
jectories of captured Higgsinos as radial when calculating
their subsequent cooling. The threshold for scattering on
uranium determines the stall turning pointR stall:

We now compare the predicted Higgsino annihilation
rates in the Sun with IceCube limits on the DM annihi-

benchmark (mχ ≃1.1 TeV) and nonthermal scenarios

the annihilation-rate limit Γlim from IceCube’s published

cooling, which is a more realistic scenario. Atm χ =
10 TeV, for instance, we obtainδ≳390 keV, substan-
tially weaker than the approximately 510 keV bound in-
ferred from Ref. [13], which assumes a population ther-
malized with the solar core. The Sommerfeld resonance
nearm χ≃6.5 TeV also produces a visible strengthening
of these bounds.
Our estimates of the IceCube limits for Higgsino
masses between 0.1 and 200 TeV illustrate their strong
dependence on the assumed elastic interactions and the
evolution of the captured population. Our cooling-time
estimates should nevertheless be interpreted with cau-
tion, as our calculation treats solar nuclei as stationary.
In particular, protons in the solar core have appreciable
thermal velocities, which can increase the elastic SD col-
lision rate relative to the stationary-target estimate when
Higgsino speeds are small, without changing the under-
lying cross section. Scattering from a moving nucleus
can transfer energy either to or from a Higgsino, and
a consistent treatment of kinetic relaxation must include
both processes. The stationary-nuclei approximation, to-
gether with the radial-orbit, uniform-density, and fixed-
effective-volume assumptions, limits the quantitative reli-
ability of our cooling-dependent bounds, especially when
the cooling time is comparable to the solar age.
To summarize, we found four variants of IceCube
bounds on Higgsino parameter space. The most con-
straining gold-dashed curve makes the unrealistic as-
sumption of s capture–annihilation equilibrium for all
Higgsino masses, whereas the other three variants ac-
tually compute the annihilation rate under three dif-
ferent assumptions about the Higgsino–nucleon scatter-
ing, which determines the final radius to which the Hig-
gsinos cool and thus the final annihilation rate. The
three variants of bounds we find are: the green curve
when we include all three types of interactions (SI in-
elastic, SD elastic, and SI elastic), the purple solid curve
when we include inelastic and SD elastic, and the ma-
genta dot-dashed curve when we include only the inelas-
tic scattering. We note that the purple solid and ma-
genta dot-dashed curves are essentially identical for Hig-
gsino masses above 5 TeV. We believe the gold-dashed
curve above 10 TeV and the magenta dot-dashed curve
below 5 TeV to be too extreme to be realistic: the gold-
dashed curve assumes capture–annihilation equilibrium
for all masses and the magenta dot-dashed curve does
not take into account any elastic scattering at all. The
green and purple solid curves outline reasonable bounds
on Higgsino parameter space.

In this section, we compare the Higgsino parameter
space compatible with the LZ event of interest [5] with
the IceCube constraints derived from Higgsino capture
and annihilation in the Sun. The regions compatible
with these experiments are shown in Fig. 2 in the (δ,mχ)
plane.
Building on our earlier paper [6], we extend our analy-
sis to Higgsino masses above the thermal relic benchmark
(mχ≃1.1 TeV). Using the approximate likelihood de-
scribed above, we perform a likelihood-ratio analysis to
determine the 90% CL regions in the (δ,m χ) plane, both
with and without the high-energy LZ sideband. In Fig. 2,
the orange regions show the results obtained without the
sideband, while the blue regions include the constraint
from the absence of events in this energy range. The
upper panel assumes the Standard Halo Model (SHM),
while the lower panel includes the enhancement of the
high-speed tail due to the LMC. The IceCube bounds at
90% CL discussed in the previous section are also shown
for comparison.
We first consider the (unrealistic for masses above
10 TeV) limiting assumption of capture–annihilation
equilibrium for all Higgsino masses, shown in the gold-
dashed curve, which gives the strongest IceCube bounds
for a given capture rate. Even under this assumption,
regions compatible with both LZ and IceCube remain,
whether or not the sideband is included. For the SHM,
the overlap lies atm χ ≳80 TeV, with 340 keV≲
δ≲390 keV. For the SHM + LMC distribution, it
spans approximately 13 TeV≲m χ ≲120 TeV, with
465 keV≲δ≲520 keV. Mass and splitting remain
correlated within these regions. Capture–annihilation
equilibrium over the full Higgsino mass range is consid-
ered in Ref. [15], which identifies regions compatible with
both LZ and IceCube at Higgsino masses of order 100–
1000 TeV within its 68% confidence contours. This mass
range does not by itself indicate a discrepancy with our
results, since we present 90% confidence regions. Indeed,
the broader 2σcontours in Ref. [15], corresponding to
95% CL, allow lower masses and show qualitative agree-
ment with our 90% confidence regions when capture–
annihilation equilibrium is imposed.
We next compare the LZ contours with the IceCube
limits obtained by accounting for different cooling chan-
nels without imposing capture–annihilation equilibrium.
As discussed in Sec. VI, the assumed interactions con-
tributing to cooling can significantly affect the IceCube
limits. The three interactions considered are inelastic SI
scattering, which governs Higgsino–nucleus scattering in
xenon-based detectors, and elastic SD and SI scattering,
which are subdominant in direct detection.
The green dot-dashed curve shows our results when
we include all three interactions in the cooling. For the
SHM, compatibility with LZ and IceCube requires ap-
proximatelym χ ≳30 TeV, with mass splittings roughly
between 350 and 400 keV, regardless of whether the high-
energy sideband is included. For SHM + LMC, the com-
patible region differs only little from that obtained by
imposing capture–annihilation equilibrium which is dis-
cussed in the previous paragraph.
We then explore a scenario in which the elastic SI inter-
action is negligible, as allowed by theoretical uncertain-
ties [30]. Here, elastic SD scattering plays a central role in
cooling. Its cross section scales asσ SD,elastic∝m−2
χ in the
heavy-mass limit [16] 10 and therefore becomes consider-
ably weaker above the thermal Higgsino mass, increasing
the cooling time and weakening the IceCube limits. For
the SHM + LMC velocity distribution, Higgsinos with
masses as low as approximately 2 TeV andδ∼520 keV
remain compatible with both experiments (LZ and Ice-
Cube) at 90% CL if the high-energy sideband is omit-
ted. For the SHM, the overlap occurs atm χ ≳10 TeV,
with mass splittings roughly between 370 and 400 keV,
whether or not the sideband constraint is included.
Finally, we consider cooling through inelastic SI scat-
tering alone, neglecting both elastic channels. This sce-
nario is not realistic for Higgsino masses below 5 TeV
where the elastic scattering cannot be neglected. In this
limiting case, the thermal Higgsino is excluded for the
SHM but remains marginally compatible with the Ice-
Cube limits and the LZ event at 90% CL for SHM +
LMC andδ∼508 keV, provided the high-energy side-
band is omitted. Hence this is the only case in which the
thermal Higgsino remains a viable explanation of the LZ
data.
In summary, regions compatible with both LZ and
IceCube remain for all cooling assumptions considered
here, whether or not the high-energy LZ sideband is in-
cluded. Depending on the halo distribution and cool-
ing interactions, these regions occur at Higgsino masses
from∼2 TeV to tens of TeV or higher. Obtaining
the observed DM abundance at these masses requires an
abundance history beyond standard radiation-dominated
freeze-out [15].
Although the 1.1 TeV thermal Higgsino is only
marginally viable in the extreme case where all elastic
scattering cooling mechansims are neglected, a Higgsino
mass as low as∼2 TeV remains a good candidate. It
provides an explanation of the LZ event and remains con-
sistent with IceCube bounds when we make reasonable
assumptions about cooling, when LMC enhancement is
included (but ignoring the empty LZ sideband).
VIII. DISCUSSION
In this paper, we have explored thermal and nonther-
mal Higgsino dark-matter interpretations of the 248±

observed by LZ [5], expanding upon our earlier work [6].
The event lies in a region of low expected background,
and none of the rare background processes or detector ef-
fects investigated by the LZ Collaboration was identified
as a likely explanation. In this work, we have considered
the enhancement of the local DM high-speed tail due to
the Large Magellanic Cloud (LMC), the constraint from
the absence of events in the high-energy LZ sideband, and
DM-annihilation constraints from IceCube under differ-
ent assumptions about Higgsino cooling and annihilation
in the Sun. Our main results are summarized in Fig. 2,
where regions in the Higgsino parameter space compat-
ible with both the LZ event and IceCube bounds are
presented.
We find that the thermal Higgsino withm χ≃1.1 TeV
is incompatible with the combination of LZ and IceCube
if we assume the Standard Halo Model (SHM) only. For
the SHM + LMC distribution which accounts for the
enhanced high-speed tail of the DM velocity distribu-
tion, thermal Higgsino remains marginally compatible
nearδ≃508 keV only when both SI and SD elastic cool-
ing channels are neglected and the high-energy sideband
is omitted. Since the loop-induced elastic SD interaction
contributes considerably to cooling atm χ ≃1.1 TeV,
this possibility represents a limiting case rather than a
robust thermal-Higgsino interpretation.
Heavier Higgsinos allow compatibility with both
datasets at 90% confidence level without the above re-
strictive assumptions needed in the thermal Higgsino in-
terpretation. Depending on the halo distribution and
cooling channels, the surviving regions extend from Hig-
gsino masses∼2 TeV to above several hundred TeV.
Although the parameter range we studied only went up
to Higgsino masses of 200 TeV, it is clear from our
results that higher mass Higgsinos will be compatible
with LZ and IceCube bounds as well. The relevant
splittings are approximately 350–400 keV for the SHM
and 460–525 keV for SHM + LMC, with the mass and
splitting correlated within each region. Compatible re-
gions remain even in the most extreme case considered,
when the high-energy sideband is included and capture–
annihilation equilibrium (which yields the strongest Ice-
Cube limits at a fixed capture rate but is unrealistic for
mχ >10 TeV) is imposed. Obtaining the observed DM
abundance at these masses requires an abundance history
beyond standard radiation-dominated freeze-out [15].
The DM-annihilation constraints depend on whether
the captured Higgsinos cool efficiently enough to ap-
proach capture–annihilation equilibrium. The decrease
of the elastic SD cross section asm −2
χ in the heavy-
mass limit [16] can substantially reduce cooling at large
masses. Consequently, imposing equilibrium throughout
the parameter space can overestimate the annihilation
rate. We also show that Sommerfeld enhancement can re-
store stronger bounds near resonant masses, but, within
our population-evolution model, these bounds cannot ex-
ceed those obtained when we have capture-annihilation
equilibrium for a fixed capture rate.
Several uncertainties affect the comparison with LZ.
Inelastic scattering at the splittings considered here
probes the high-speed tail of the local DM distribution,
making the inferred parameter regions sensitive to its
shape and normalization. We took into account the ef-
fects of the LMC on the high velocity tail by using a
particular model (“halo-13”) in the work of [33]. As
discussed in Section IV, the SHM + LMC distribution
remains uncertain; further, other massive bodies in the
MW may have effects not yet taken into account.
Another question is whether or not to impose the con-
straint of an empty high energy recoil LZ sideband on our
model. The sideband constraint depends on the assumed
signal acceptance, which has not been published for this
region. Our approximate likelihood therefore cannot re-
place a dedicated analysis by the LZ Collaboration. The
analysis in this paper is performed with and without tak-
ing into account the empty sideband.
The solar capture calculation has additional uncertain-
ties associated with the abundance and radial distribu-
tion of heavy elements. For species without individual
radial profiles in our adopted solar model, we assume
constant mass fractions (an assumption also adopted in
Ref. [12]), so that their number densities follow the to-
tal solar density [12]. Although their normalizations are
based on the abundances in Ref. [43], these inputs do
not determine their interior profiles. This approximation
matters because heavy nuclei provide the targets needed
to overcome the inelastic threshold. Nuclear form-factor
uncertainties can affect both Higgsino capture and cool-
ing, with their impact on elastic scattering illustrated
in Ref. [30]. These effects are distinct from the scalar–
spin-2 cancellation in the loop-induced elastic SI ampli-
tude [30], which significantly affects Higgsino cooling in
the Sun and consequently the IceCube bound on Hig-
gsino DM. The combined impact of these uncertainties
on the inferred bounds has not been quantified.
The evolution of the captured population is another
important approximation. We describe cooling through
a characteristic orbital radius and approximate the fi-
nal population as a uniform sphere. Our annihilation
calculation uses the resulting final spatial and velocity
distributions rather than evolving cooling and annihila-
tion together for particles captured at different times.
When cooling is slow, assigning the final distribution to
the entire population can overestimate its central concen-
tration and thereby lead to strengthen the inferred Ice-
Cube bounds. A calculation that follows the spatial and
velocity distributions throughout capture, cooling, and
annihilation would provide a more reliable treatment of
these regions.
Finally, our conversion of the predicted annihilation
rate into an IceCube constraint uses the published
W +W− channel as an approximation to the Higgsino
neutrino signal. A dedicated calculation would account
for the Higgsino annihilation branching fractions, neu-
trino propagation through the Sun, and the detector re-
sponse. The IceCube limits on theW +W− channel used

here extend to 10 TeV [40]; above this mass, we hold
the inferred annihilation-rate limit fixed at its 10 TeV
value. The resulting high-mass bounds and compatibil-

Complementary observations can further test the Hig-
gsino DM interpretation. Super-Kamiokande data also
constrain neutrinos from solar Higgsino annihilation, al-
though the limits reported in Ref. [49] are weaker than
those from IceCube. Paleodetectors are a proposed ex-

readout of DM-induced nuclear recoil tracks in natu-
ral minerals [50–53]. Heavy-element paleodetectors of-

ing through recoil tracks in ancient minerals [54]. To-
gether with additional high-energy recoil data and im-
proved solar-neutrino calculations, such searches can test
the parameter regions identified here.
